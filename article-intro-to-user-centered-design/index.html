Introduction to iterative user-centered design

Problem

Let’s imagine you design an app. You create a nicely looking interface mockup. You don’t show it anyone who will use it. Developers implement it. Users try to use it… and they react like this:

You realize that your interface is difficult to use and causes frustration or friction. You wasted thousands of $$$ to develop an app that users don’t love. Now you need spend more to fix it.

Solution: prototyping and testing

The solution is simple: you need to show the interface to the user before spending money on development. You want to ensure your design is user-friendly, during the design, not after.

It’s called “user-centered design” because you introduce user to each design phase.

You create a first version and create a cheap prototype. You show it to potential users and “test” it. Then you improve it and “test” it again. And again… Until you feel confident that the design is user-friendly.

Introducing user to all phases of design

Interviewing Users & Stakeholders

Even before creating a first draft of the interface, you want to meet users and ask them about the problems you want to solve with your software. Here you interview users without a prototype.

Prototyping

Once you have a rough idea, you create a first version of a prototype. The prototype doesn’t need to look well. It should have basic interactions and copy It just needs to be something that you can show to the users.

User testing

Then you meet with the users. We call such meetings “usability tests” because you test your prototype (you can see how it looks like here). You show them a prototype and ask to imagine that they use it.

If you don’t have access to users, you can just meet with someone else who might be the user in the future. This can be your mom, husband or wife (”friends & family”) or your colleagues from other departments that don’t work on software development.

Fixing prototypes

During the user testing, you realise that your first idea is not the best. You spot problems, that you hadn’t thought of before. Now it’s time to fix them. You update the prototype, and go back to user testing.

User testing again (and again…)

Once you fix some problems, you may spot new problems. You need to decide when your design is ready and good enough with acceptable risk. Usually after 5 usability tests you discovered the most critical problems, and you’re safe to move on to the development phase.

Visual UI & Specs

Now it’s the right time to spend time on pixel-perfectness. You deliberately didn’t do it before, because you wanted to focus on changing the prototype, and not push pixels again and again.

Coding & release

Once you release your software your users will most likely be happy 🤞

Don’t draw any statistical conclusions

User-centered design helps you spot problems and solve them ahead of time, but you only talk to 5 people. This is too small sample size to draw any statistical conclusions. Don’t count users and say “80% of testers had no problems with my design”.

During usability tests we focus on “Why” and not “How many”. That’s why this type of research is called qualitative, not quantitative.

Discovering user problems

Even if you observed a problem in just 1 usability test, this might still be a “discovery”. When you understand the reason behind it, this will show you a direction to a better design. If more than 2 people encounter the same usability issue, you can assume that this particular problem is not isolated to one specific personality.

Types of usability testing

  • Informal “corridor” tests - when you ask your college for opinion, or friends and family
  • Semi-formal - when you have a simple testing scenario and you invite right people but you don’t need academic perfectness (for example you invite real clients or have similar computer skills to potential buyers)
  • Formal test - when you organise a full-blown research sessions with cameras and measurements and spend lots of time for research preparations
  • Biased formal tests - when you organise a full-blown research, but you choose the wrong people or make methodological mistakes anyway, or it’s too late to make changes in the design (by the way, semi-formal tests can also be biased, but they are still better because you spend less money on them)

From our experience, semi-formal tests are the best value for money - they provide good insights and minimise research bias without too much effort required.

Common myths about usability testing

MythTruth
It will make our development process slowerNo, it will make it faster because you will be sure that you are developing a right thing from the beginning and avoid debates and deadlocks
I know what my users want and I don’t need testsNo, you are not a user. You spend 8 hours a day working on your product but user gives you only several minutes of their total attention. Users have their own own life that is not engaged in your business. Everyone is different.
It is expensiveNo, semi formal tests are not expensive. 
And it is 10 times less expensive than wasting money on development of bad usability and fixing usability issues post development.
I have years of experience in design, I know what I’m doingYears of experience will not grant you telepathic abilities. You still can’t guess how brains of others are functioning. Every human is unique.
